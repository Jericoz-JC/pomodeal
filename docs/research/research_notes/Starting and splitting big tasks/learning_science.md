# Learning science: starting aversive tasks and splitting big ones into segments people finish

Scope: behavioural and learning-science evidence for Pomodeal (phone-first study timer that negotiates breaks; task types are writing, problem sets and coding, exam study, open-ended projects).

Source note: links marked (†) are DOI links to well-established papers cited from the existing literature. They were **not re-fetched in this session**, so figures attached to them should be spot-checked before they go into public copy. Links without (†) were read or surfaced by search in this session. Populations are noted in [brackets].

## 1. Task initiation: what helps people start aversive tasks?

### Takeaway
The best-supported starting tools are **if-then plans (implementation intentions)** and **making the first action concrete**. Making the deadline or reward feel near (temporal motivation theory), a concrete first step, and self-compassion after a lapse are moderately supported. Temptation bundling and self-set deadlines work, but with small or decaying effects. The fresh-start effect is mostly archival and has a documented backfire mode. Procrastination is best modelled as **short-term mood repair** (avoiding a bad feeling now), not laziness or a time-management deficit. Design should therefore lower the emotional cost of the first minute, not add pressure.

### Cited Findings

**Why people delay (theory)**
- Temporal motivation theory (TMT) says motivation ≈ (expectancy × value) / (1 + impulsiveness × delay). Procrastination rises as a task's rewards get more distant, as the task gets more aversive, and as a person's impulsivity rises. Steel's meta-analysis (691 correlations) found that task aversiveness, task delay, low self-efficacy, impulsiveness and low conscientiousness were the strongest correlates. It also estimated that 80–95% of college students procrastinate and about half do so consistently and problematically [mostly student samples]. — [Steel 2007, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65); [Steel & König 2006, Academy of Management Review (†)](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2006.22527462)
- Procrastination is framed as the **"priority of short-term mood repair"**: people avoid the negative feeling the task produces now, at the cost of their future self. — [Sirois & Pychyl 2013, Social & Personality Psychology Compass (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011)
- A classic longitudinal study (older, 1997) found that student procrastinators had less stress early in the semester and more stress, more illness and lower grades by the end. The short-term relief is real, and so is the long-term cost. — [Tice & Baumeister 1997, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00460.x)
- Intentions alone do little. In a meta-analysis of experimental studies, a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36): the intention–behaviour gap. — [Webb & Sheeran 2006, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249)

**Implementation intentions (if-then plans)**
- Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (>8,000 participants): forming "If situation X, then I will do Y" plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65). Getting started (initiation) was one of the problems they addressed best. — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology (†)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1); theory in [Gollwitzer 1999, American Psychologist (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493)
- Procrastination-intervention meta-analyses: randomized trials against inactive controls give a small overall effect, g = 0.34 [95% CI 0.11, 0.56], with significant heterogeneity. Few controlled studies exist for most specific techniques. — [Rozental et al. 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588/pdf). A separate meta-analysis (12 RCTs, N = 646) reported g = 1.18, but effects were larger with in-person delivery, student samples and no-treatment controls, so it is likely inflated. — [Malouff & Schutte 2019, J. Counseling & Development](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/jcad.12243)

**Concrete first step / "just start"**
- Construal level: when students were asked to think about a task in **concrete** terms (how to do it) rather than abstract terms (why), they completed and returned it sooner [students; online tasks with deadlines]. — [McCrea, Liberman, Trope & Sherman 2008, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02240.x)
- Students who mentally rehearsed the **process** of studying (when, where, how) started earlier, studied more hours and scored higher on a midterm than students who imagined the **outcome** (getting a good grade). Outcome-only simulation did not help [UCLA undergraduates; older, 1999]. — [Pham & Taylor 1999, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167299025002010)
- The **Ovsiankina effect** (once started and interrupted, people tend to resume a task) is supported by a 2025 meta-analysis. This is the best evidence that "just starting" creates its own pull. See Section 2. — [Ghibellini & Meier 2025, Humanities & Social Sciences Communications](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)

**Time framing and deadlines**
- **Task categorization of time:** people start earlier when a deadline falls in the *same* time category as now (for example "this month" or "this week") than when an equally distant deadline falls in the next category, even with the same number of days left [multiple studies including field data; single research team]. — [Tu & Soman 2014, Journal of Consumer Research (†)](https://doi.org/10.1086/677840)
- **Self-imposed deadlines:** students allowed to set their own deadlines for three papers did set binding ones, and performed better than students with one final deadline. They performed worse than students given evenly spaced external deadlines, because they did not space their own deadlines well [MIT executive-education students and paid proofreaders; small N; 2002]. — [Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00441) **Retracted September 2026** ([retraction notice](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261488042)); do not cite.

**Temptation bundling**
- Original field experiment (N = 226): allowing tempting audiobooks only at the gym raised gym visits by about 51% at first, but the effect decayed over time, especially after Thanksgiving. Many participants said they would pay for gym-only access to the audiobooks (demand for commitment). — [Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014, Management Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784); decay description via [Kirgios et al. summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:161:y:2020:i:s:p:20-35)
- Large follow-up (N = 6,792; 4-week program): an audiobook plus encouragement to temptation-bundle raised the probability of a weekly workout by 10–14%. Encouragement alone had only a small effect, and it worked on the extensive margin (whether people went at all, not how often). — [Kirgios, Mandel, Park, Milkman et al. 2020, OBHDP](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:161:y:2020:i:s:p:20-35); [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/37902)
- Megastudy: 54 four-week interventions with 61,293 gym members; reported gains in weekly visits ranged from 9% to 27% for effective arms. — [Milkman et al. 2021, Nature (†)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-04128-4) (figures via secondary source in search; check against paper)

**Fresh-start effect**
- Archival field studies: Google searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments rise after temporal landmarks (new week, month, birthday, holiday). The proposed mechanism is mental accounting: the landmark pushes past failures into an "old self" period. — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901); [author PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)
- Landmarks spur goal initiation specifically when they are framed as new beginnings. — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2015, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615605818)
- Backfire: Dai's dissertation notes that fresh starts motivate goal pursuit "and when they backfire". Later work reports that an *anticipated* landmark can reduce effort on goals already in progress ("intrapersonal loafing": "I'll restart Monday"). — [Dai dissertation, Penn](https://repository.upenn.edu/edissertations/1035/); loafing claim surfaced in search snippet attributed to Koo, Dai, Mai & Song (in press), not verified.

**Self-compassion and self-forgiveness after procrastinating**
- First-year psychology students (N ≈ 119 analysed) who forgave themselves for procrastinating before midterm 1 procrastinated less before midterm 2. The effect held only among heavy procrastinators and ran through reduced negative affect. Design: correlational, self-report, two time points. — [Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett 2010, Personality and Individual Differences, via BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cure-procrastination-forgive-yourself); no direct replication found ([Silicon Canals summary](https://siliconcanals.com/t-self-forgiveness-procrastination-two-midterms-carleton/))
- Experimental support for the mechanism: after failing a difficult test, students induced to be self-compassionate spent *more* time studying for a retest than students given a self-esteem boost or no manipulation [undergraduates]. — [Breines & Chen 2012, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599)
- Lower self-compassion partly explains the link between procrastination and stress [students and community adults; correlational]. — [Sirois 2014, Self and Identity (†)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298868.2013.763404)

### Inferences
- The product's "negotiation" framing fits the mood-repair account: planning the break you'll take anyway makes the avoidance reward explicit and time-boxed instead of shameful. TMT supports making the payoff close and visible.
- Implementation intentions are the strongest single lever and cost almost nothing in UI: one "when/where → first move" field on the deal ticket.
- "Just start" is best supported indirectly (concrete construal, process simulation, Ovsiankina resumption), not by any trial of "2-minute rules". The product can promote a tiny first move, but should not claim a specific minute count is backed by research.
- Self-forgiveness evidence is thin (one correlational study) but agrees with stronger self-compassion experiments. It is a good fit for the no-shame rule, but copy should not promise outcomes.
- Fresh starts are a two-edged tool: useful for re-entry after a lapse, harmful if they let users defer ("start Monday"). Offer landmarks only when the user is already off-track, never as an alternative to starting now.

### Gaps
- **Verified 2026-11-08 against the full text:** Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2025), European Review of Social Psychology 36(1), 162-194, [doi:10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563). 642 tests; weighted average d = .36 [.33, .40]; trim-and-fill d = .35 [.31, .38]; RoBMA d = .15 [.08, .22] with extreme evidence for publication bias. Behaviour change d = .27; if-then format d = .43 vs schedule .29; time and place d = .46.
- Implementation intentions for academic procrastination specifically (for example Owens, Bowman & Dill 2008) were not verified this session.
- No pre-registered replication of the fresh-start field studies was found. The evidence is archival and from the original team.
- No controlled trial of "tiny first step" or "2-minute rule" interventions was found. These claims come from popular books (for example Fogg, Clear), not peer-reviewed tests.
- The 2018 van Eerde & Klingsieck intervention meta-analysis (Educational Research Review) was not retrieved, so its moderator results (for example on CBT) are missing.

## 2. Segmentation of big tasks (2–4 h, spread over days)

### Takeaway
Breaking a big task into **near-term subgoals** helps both motivation and completion. The classic evidence is strong but old. Unpacking a task into steps also corrects the planning fallacy by making people estimate more time. Progress display should emphasize whichever part is *smaller* (done early on, remaining later on). **Stopping mid-task with a known next step is supported by the Ovsiankina resumption effect, not by the Zeigarnik memory effect, which did not replicate.** For exam study, spacing and retrieval practice are among the most robust findings in learning science.

### Cited Findings

**Proximal subgoals**
- Children (ages 7–10) weak in subtraction who set **proximal** goals (complete 6 pages per session) progressed faster, gained more skill and self-efficacy, and developed more intrinsic interest than children with a **distal** goal (42 pages over 7 sessions) or no goal [children; older classic, 1981]. — [Bandura & Schunk 1981, JPSP (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586)
- Goal-setting theory: specific, challenging goals beat "do your best". On complex, novel tasks, though, **specific learning goals** (find strategies, learn X) beat performance goals, and proximal goals help on such tasks. — [Locke & Latham 2002, American Psychologist (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)

**Goal gradient and progress framing**
- Goal-gradient effect: coffee-card customers bought coffee more often as they got closer to a free drink. A 12-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled was completed faster than an equivalent blank 10-stamp card (the "illusion of progress"). — [Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, Journal of Marketing Research (†)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)
- **Small-area hypothesis:** people are more motivated when attention goes to whichever is smaller: progress made (early in a goal) or progress remaining (late in a goal) [loyalty-program field data and lab studies]. — [Koo & Fishbach 2012, Journal of Consumer Research (†)](https://doi.org/10.1086/663827)
- **Progress principle:** in about 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 knowledge workers in 7 companies, making progress on meaningful work was the event most associated with good "inner work life" (emotions, motivation, perceptions) on a given day. Even small wins counted [correlational diary study; knowledge workers, not students]. — [Amabile & Kramer 2011, Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)

**Planning fallacy and unpacking**
- Students predicted they would finish their honours theses in about 34 days on average. They actually took about 56 days, and a minority finished by their own predicted date [students; older classic, 1994]. — [Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994, JPSP (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366)
- **Unpacking** a multi-step task into its components led to longer, more realistic completion-time predictions and reduced the optimistic bias. This held especially for complex tasks with many steps [students; multiple studies]. — [Kruger & Evans 2004, JESP (†)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001)
- **Segmentation effect:** across three experiments, the sum of time allocated to subtasks was larger than the time allocated to the same task as a whole, and rounding did not explain it. The authors discuss segmentation as a fix for planning-fallacy bias. Caveat: the tasks were short, lasting minutes rather than hours. — [Forsyth & Burt 2008, Memory & Cognition](https://dx.doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.4.791); generalization caveat in [Princeton-hosted paper](https://ourapps.deptcpanel.princeton.edu/uploads/190805145602-A-7320.pdf)
- "Unit asking" (asking first about one unit, then the total) raised charitable donations. It is a pricing/donation finding, and its transfer to time planning is untested. — [Hsee, Zhang, Lu & Xu 2013, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613482947)

**Zeigarnik / Ovsiankina and stopping mid-task ("park downhill")**
- A 2025 meta-analysis found **no memory advantage** for unfinished tasks (the Zeigarnik effect "lacks universal validity"). It did find **a general tendency to resume** interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect). The authors suggest Zeigarnik's original results depended on experimenter authority, task demands and involvement. — [Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html). Secondary summaries report a recall ratio around 0.99 (no difference) and about 67% resumption against a 50% baseline. **These numbers were not verified against the paper.** — [Bern PDF summary](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf)
- A related Bern thesis combined meta-analysis with new replication attempts: it found a resumption tendency but no memory advantage. — [Ghibellini thesis, BORIS](https://boristheses.unibe.ch/4796/)
- The "Hemingway trick" (stop when you know what comes next) is anecdote. No direct experimental test was found. Its plausible mechanisms are Ovsiankina resumption plus lowering the cost of the next start (concrete next action; see McCrea 2008 in Section 1).

**Distributed vs massed work; retrieval; interleaving (exam study)**
- Spacing: a meta-analysis (839 assessments, 317 experiments) found that distributed practice beats massed practice for verbal recall. — [Cepeda et al. 2006, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). The optimal gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember: roughly 10–20% of the retention interval for about 1-week to 1-year retention. — [Cepeda et al. 2008, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)
- A review of 10 study techniques rated **practice testing** and **distributed practice** "high utility". It rated rereading, highlighting and summarization "low utility". — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- Retrieval practice meta-analyses: testing beats restudy, about g = 0.50 [lab studies] — [Rowland 2014, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559); about g = 0.61 including classroom studies — [Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, Review of Educational Research (†)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)
- Successive relearning (retrieve to a criterion of correct recall, then relearn across several spaced sessions) produces large, durable gains in real courses [college students]. — [Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013, Educational Psychology Review (†)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4)
- Interleaving meta-analysis: overall g ≈ 0.42. It is larger for visual category learning (for example paintings), smaller for mathematics, and **negative** for word lists. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209). In a classroom RCT [7th-grade math], interleaved practice roughly doubled test scores one month later compared with blocked practice. — [Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015, Journal of Educational Psychology (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001)

### Inferences
- Every big task should be split at planning time into segments that each fit one or two focus blocks. Showing a "segmented estimate vs gut estimate" comparison uses the unpacking and segmentation effects to correct optimism without lecturing.
- "Park downhill" is a sound feature if it is framed as **making the next start easy** (a written next step), not as "your brain will keep thinking about it". The Zeigarnik memory claim should not appear in copy.
- Progress UI: show stamps earned while under about half done; switch to "segments left" past half (small-area hypothesis). This fits the existing stamped-ticket metaphor.
- For multi-day tasks, the spacing evidence argues for at least two separate days for any exam-study task. For writing and projects, the evidence base is distributed *work* (Boice-style brief regular sessions) rather than distributed *learning*. The two are related but not the same evidence.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on segmentation for multi-hour, multi-day student assignments specifically. The planning-fallacy segmentation evidence uses short tasks.
- Amabile's progress principle is correlational and from workplace samples. No student RCT of "progress logging" was found in this pass.
- I did not verify the exact Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effect sizes from the full text, which is paywalled.

## 3. Timer structure: Pomodoro vs flexible breaks, break content, time perception, body doubling

### Takeaway
There is **no clear winner** between Pomodoro, Flowtime and self-regulated breaks in student studies. One study favoured systematic breaks for mood and efficiency; a later, larger one found no overall difference. Micro-breaks reliably reduce fatigue (small effects) but do not reliably improve performance on demanding work. **Phone-based breaks** may undo the recovery value of a break (one experiment). Timing difficulties in ADHD are supported by meta-analysis, which backs externalizing time. Body doubling evidence is **thin**: small samples, self-report and one null controlled study.

### Cited Findings

**Pomodoro vs self-regulated vs Flowtime**
- Biwer et al. 2023 [87 Dutch university students, real study sessions]: self-regulated breaks (n = 35) vs 24/6 "Pomodoro" (n = 25) vs 12/3 (n = 27). Self-regulators had longer study sessions *and* longer breaks, with more fatigue and distractedness and lower concentration and motivation. There was **no difference in mental effort or task completion**. The authors conclude that systematic breaks had mood benefits and apparent efficiency benefits. — [Biwer et al. 2023, British Journal of Educational Psychology (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)
- Smits, Wenzel & de Bruin 2025 [94 Dutch psychology students, 93% women, 2-hour authentic online session; randomized]:
  - **Conditions:** self-regulated (n = 25), Pomodoro 4×25/5 (n = 36), and **Flowtime** (n = 33). In Flowtime, students chose when to break, and the break length followed the block: ≤25 min → 5 min break, 25–50 min → 8 min, >50 min → 10 min.
  - **Time use was similar:** about 1.7 h of study per group, with 3.04, 4.00 and 2.82 breaks respectively.
  - **No main effects:** no group differences in motivation, productivity, fatigue, task completion (70%, 69% and 67%; p = .854) or flow.
  - **Slopes differed:** Pomodoro fatigue rose faster, and Pomodoro motivation fell faster (about 2% odds per minute).
  - **Conclusion:** "no one of these break-taking techniques should be more highly recommended over the others". The authors speculate that fixed breaks of suboptimal length may explain the steeper slopes.
  - **Limitations:** single-item 4-point scales, uncontrolled break activities, a small and homogeneous sample, and participants who knew the study was about breaks.
  — [Smits et al. 2025, Behavioral Sciences (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)

**Breaks: what restores**
- Micro-break meta-analysis (breaks of 10 min or less; 22 samples, N = 2,335): small significant effects on vigor (d = .36) and fatigue (d = .35). The effect on overall performance was non-significant (d = .16, p = .116). Performance gains appeared only for **less cognitively demanding** tasks, and longer breaks gave bigger performance boosts. The authors suggest that highly demanding tasks may need breaks longer than 10 min [mostly adult workers and students]. — [Albulescu et al. 2022, PLOS ONE (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f); [ScienceDaily summary](https://Www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220831152754.htm)
- **Phone breaks:** in an experiment with 414 participants solving anagrams, those who took a mid-task break on a **cell phone** took 19% longer and solved 22% fewer problems afterwards than those whose break used a computer or paper. Their performance matched the no-break group. The break task was the same across media (a shopping list) [single study; US undergraduates]. — [Kang & Kurtzberg 2019, Journal of Behavioral Addictions, via Rutgers](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/need-mental-break-avoid-your-cell-phone-rutgers-researchers-say)
- Smits et al. note that active breaks (brief physical activity), relaxation and socializing are suggested restorative activities, and that respite self-efficacy and personality moderate break benefits. They did not measure break content. — [Smits et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)
- An alternative to "willpower depletion": the **opportunity-cost model** treats the felt cost of effort and boredom as a signal that other activities are worth more, not as a fuel tank running empty. — [Kurzban et al. 2013, Behavioral and Brain Sciences (†)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196)

**Time perception and externalizing time**
- Meta-analysis of 55 studies: broad timing deficits in people with ADHD, including time estimation and production accuracy (pointing to an "accelerated internal clock"). Time reproduction had additional deficits tied to attention and motivation. In one summary, time estimation (8 studies, N = 1,024) showed a small-to-medium effect and time discrimination a medium effect. — [Marx et al. 2022 meta-analysis (Southampton eprints)](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/456003); [em-consulte record](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1527936/meta-analysis-altered-perceptual-timing-abilities-)
- In adults specifically the evidence is scarcer and inconsistent: some studies find time-estimation, reproduction and time-management deficits, others do not, partly because methods vary. — [Weissenberger et al. 2023 review, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/)
- Barkley's model (older theory, 1997) links ADHD to weak behavioural inhibition, which in turn impairs working memory and a sense of time. This is the theoretical basis for the clinical advice to externalize time and information at the "point of performance". — [Barkley 1997, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.1.65)

**Body doubling and co-presence**
- VR study (12 adults with ADHD; alone vs human vs AI body double): participants finished faster and rated their sustained attention higher with a body double and preferred having one. Opinions diverged, and the paper says earlier evidence was inconclusive. — [arXiv 2509.12153](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12153v1)
- A null result: a small brain-computer interface study (unmedicated ADHD, medicated ADHD and neurotypical groups) found no effect of body doubling on performance, focus or calm. The authors note their task may not match real use, which is mainly *starting* tasks. — [CCSC Central Plains 2025 poster](https://www.ccsc.org/centralplains/2025/talks/talk-poster16/)
- Survey of 220 people, mostly neurodivergent: body doubling is used for starting tasks and for reducing isolation, not only for productivity. — [ACM, doi 10.1145/3689648 (as surfaced in search)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3689648); a research-agenda framework paper exists as [arXiv 2605.07851](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07851)
- Older adjacent evidence on social facilitation (a classic meta-analysis): the presence of others modestly speeds **simple / well-learned** tasks and impairs **complex / novel** tasks. — [Bond & Titus 1983, Psychological Bulletin (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.94.2.265)

### Inferences
- The negotiation model is well matched to this evidence. Since no break schedule clearly wins, a **systematic default the user can renegotiate** is defensible: it keeps Biwer's mood benefits and the flexibility Smits suggests matters. Pomodoro's fixed 25/5 has no special evidential status.
- Break *length* should probably scale with block length and demand (the Flowtime rule and the Albulescu meta-regression). A 5-minute break after 60 minutes of writing is likely too short.
- Planned "10 min of Reels" is honest and on-brand. The Kang & Kurtzberg result suggests the app could *offer*, never require, off-phone options and help with the return transition. It should not moralize; the evidence is a single study.
- Externalizing time (always-visible elapsed and remaining time, gentle haptic checkpoints) is theoretically well motivated. Direct trials of visual timers improving adult study outcomes were not found.
- Study rooms (co-presence) should be pitched as company and a help for *starting*, not as a productivity booster. Social facilitation suggests rooms suit routine work (practice problems, flashcards) more than first drafts.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of Flowtime other than Smits 2025 was found. No study of "negotiated" or borrow-from-break schemes exists that I found.
- No controlled study was found on social-media-specific breaks (for example short-video feeds) vs other phone use during study.
- No RCT was found of external timers or time-visualization tools for adult or university study performance.
- Evidence on virtual co-working services (for example Focusmate) is vendor surveys only (for example a FLOWN survey of 117 UK adults, no control group: [neurodiversity.org summary](https://neurodiversity.org/?p=11449)).

## 4. How task types differ, and how to define "done" for fuzzy tasks

### Takeaway
"Done" is definable when the unit of work is **observable**: words or sections for writing, problems attempted for problem sets, items correctly retrieved for exam study, and decisions or artifacts for open-ended projects. Exam study has the strongest evidence for a principled done-criterion (retrieval to criterion, spaced across days). Writing has older but consistent evidence for brief regular sessions. Open-ended work suits **learning goals** and process goals over output goals.

### Cited Findings
- **Exam study:** successive relearning defines done as "each item recalled correctly to criterion, repeated across spaced sessions". It turns a fuzzy "study chapter 4" into a checkable state. — [Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4). Practice testing and spacing rate high-utility; rereading and highlighting low. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- **Problem sets and math:** interleaving problem types improves later test performance (classroom RCT) but is weaker for verbal material. — [Rohrer et al. 2015 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001); [Brunmair & Richter 2019 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)
- **Writing:** classic studies of academic writers found that brief, regular writing sessions produced more output than binge writing triggered by deadlines [faculty; older, 1989]. — [Boice 1989, Behaviour Research and Therapy (†)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(89)90144-7)
- **Open-ended and complex tasks:** for complex, novel tasks, specific *learning* goals (for example "identify three strategies" or "decide the research question") outperform performance goals. Proximal goals help on such tasks. — [Locke & Latham 2002 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)
- **All types:** concrete construal speeds initiation ([McCrea et al. 2008 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02240.x)) and process simulation beats outcome simulation for exam prep ([Pham & Taylor 1999 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167299025002010)). A concrete done-criterion per segment therefore also serves as the first-step cue.

### Inferences
Suggested default segment units and done-criteria per type:

| Type | Segment unit | Done-criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Writing | Section or paragraph | "Messy draft exists", then a separate revise segment |
| Problem sets / coding | N problems attempted, or "until stuck, then write the question I'm stuck on" | Attempted, not solved |
| Exam study | Topic | Recall each item correctly once this session; schedule the next session on another day |
| Open-ended | Decision or artifact | "Decided X" or "sketch of Y exists" |

- For coding and problem sets, "attempted plus a written stuck-point" avoids open-ended stalls and gives a natural park-downhill note.

### Gaps
- No direct studies were found comparing segmentation strategies *across* these task types within one population.
- Evidence on writing-session length for students (as opposed to faculty) is thin. Boice is older and correlational or small-sample.
- Coding-specific procrastination and segmentation research was not located in this pass.

## 5. Weak or unreplicated claims the product should not build on

### Takeaway
Avoid framing built on **ego depletion / a willpower tank**, the **Zeigarnik memory effect**, **"21 days to form a habit"**, "**Pomodoro's 25 minutes is optimal**", and **learning styles**. Treat **self-forgiveness** and **body doubling** as thin. Treat **fresh starts** as archival, with a backfire mode.

### Cited Findings
- **Ego depletion:** a 23-lab registered replication (N = 2,141) found d ≈ 0.04 — [Hagger et al. 2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873). A 36-lab pre-registered multi-site study (N = 3,531) found d ≈ 0.06 — [Vohs et al. 2021, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733). The opportunity-cost model is a better-supported alternative framing — [Kurzban et al. 2013 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196).
- **Zeigarnik memory advantage:** not supported by meta-analysis; resumption (Ovsiankina) is supported. — [Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- **Habit formation time:** median about 66 days to automaticity, with a wide range (roughly 18–254 days) [96 adult volunteers; one study]. "21 days" is not evidence-based. — [Lally et al. 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology (†)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- **Pomodoro's specific 25/5:** no evidence it beats self-regulated or Flowtime breaks on completion. — [Smits et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/); [Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)
- **Micro-breaks boosting performance on hard work:** not supported. The benefit is to fatigue and vigor. — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- **Learning styles (matching instruction to "visual or auditory learners"):** no adequate evidence supports it. — [Pashler et al. 2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest (†)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)
- **Growth-mindset interventions:** a meta-analysis found very small average effects on achievement (d ≈ 0.08). — [Sisk et al. 2018, Psychological Science (†)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704)
- **Temptation bundling:** the effect decays, and in follow-ups instruction added little beyond simply giving people the audiobook. — [Kirgios et al. 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:161:y:2020:i:s:p:20-35)
- **Self-forgiveness:** a single correlational study with no direct replication. — [BPS Research Digest on Wohl et al. 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cure-procrastination-forgive-yourself)

### Inferences
- Copy should never say "your willpower is running low" or "recharge your willpower". Say instead that "the task got more expensive than the alternatives", which matches the negotiation metaphor and the opportunity-cost model.
- Never say "unfinished tasks stay on your mind". Say "stopping with a next step makes restarting easier".
- No "21-day" or habit-streak framing. This is consistent with the no-streaks rule.

### Gaps
- "90-minute ultradian focus cycles" and "attention spans are shrinking to 8 seconds" are popular claims. No peer-reviewed support was located in this pass. Treat them as unsupported; do not use.

## Novel feature ideas

Each idea names the finding it rests on and that evidence's strength.

1. **"First move" line on the deal ticket (if-then plan).** When creating a deal, the user fills "When I sit down at ___, I'll first ___". The app pre-suggests a type-specific concrete action, for example "open the doc and write one ugly sentence" or "do problem 1 until stuck". It is shown full-screen at block start.
   - Rests on: implementation intentions (d = 0.65 in the 2006 meta; possibly smaller in newer updates), concrete construal (McCrea 2008), process simulation (Pham & Taylor 1999).
   - Strength: **strong** for if-then plans in general; **moderate** for the academic-procrastination transfer.

2. **Park-downhill re-entry note.** In the last 2 minutes of a focus block (or when the user ends early), prompt for "Next time, start by…". It becomes the next session's first move (feeds idea 1). Copy promises easier restarts, not memory effects.
   - Rests on: Ovsiankina resumption tendency (meta-analytic support), concrete construal for initiation; *explicitly not* on Zeigarnik.
   - Strength: **moderate**.

3. **Unpack-to-estimate splitter with private calibration.** For big tasks, ask for a gut estimate of the whole, then have the user split it into segments and estimate each. Show both totals side by side ("your pieces add up to 3 h 40, your gut said 2 h"). Over time the ledger records estimate vs actual per task type, privately, with no judgement. Suggested segments are sized to fit 1–2 focus blocks.
   - Rests on: planning fallacy (Buehler 1994), unpacking effect (Kruger & Evans 2004), segmentation effect (Forsyth & Burt 2008).
   - Strength: **moderate** (robust lab effects; multi-day student tasks untested).

4. **Even-spacing deadline helper for multi-day tasks.** When a task spans days, the app proposes evenly spaced self-set checkpoints instead of letting all segments pile up near the due date. Users can renegotiate any checkpoint. Exam-study tasks get a minimum of two separate days.
   - Rests on: self-imposed deadlines help but people space them poorly (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002); spacing meta-analyses (Cepeda 2006/2008).
   - Strength: **moderate** for deadlines (small N, 2002); **strong** for spacing in exam study.

5. **"This week" deadline framing.** Show due dates by period ("due this week" or "due next week") and always place the first segment inside the current period, so the task feels like a now-task.
   - Rests on: categorization of time and task initiation (Tu & Soman 2014); TMT delay term (Steel 2007).
   - Strength: **moderate** (single research team; mechanism consistent with TMT).

6. **Type-specific "done" stamps.** Each segment gets a done-criterion template by task type:
   - Writing: "messy draft of section" (revise is a separate segment).
   - Problem sets: "attempted N + wrote my stuck question".
   - Exam study: "recalled each item once without notes".
   - Open-ended: "decided ___".

   A segment's stamp lands only when the user confirms the criterion, which makes fuzzy tasks finishable. Exam-study segments offer a quick self-quiz check instead of "read chapter".
   - Rests on: retrieval practice and successive relearning (Rowland 2014; Adesope 2017; Rawson 2013), learning goals for complex tasks (Locke & Latham 2002), proximal subgoals (Bandura & Schunk 1981).
   - Strength: **strong** for exam study; **moderate** for writing and open-ended.

7. **Small-area progress display.** Before the halfway point, the ticket shows stamps earned ("3 done"). After halfway, it shows what is left ("2 to go"). A single pre-stamp is granted at planning time for having split the task, as an honest "illusion of progress", since splitting is real work.
   - Rests on: small-area hypothesis (Koo & Fishbach 2012), goal gradient and endowed progress (Kivetz et al. 2006), progress principle (Amabile & Kramer 2011, correlational).
   - Strength: **moderate**.

8. **Break length that scales with the block, renegotiable.** The default break grows with the preceding focus length and the task type, using the Flowtime rule (≤25 → 5, 25–50 → 8, >50 → 10 min) as a starting point, with a longer offer after demanding writing or coding blocks. Systematic by default; the user can renegotiate within the existing terms.
   - Rests on: Biwer 2023 (systematic breaks: mood benefit), Smits 2025 (no winner; fixed lengths may be suboptimal), Albulescu 2022 (longer breaks help on demanding tasks).
   - Strength: **moderate** (two small student RCTs plus a meta-analysis on micro-breaks).

9. **Return ramp after phone breaks, with no moralizing.** The planned "10 min of Reels" stays fully allowed. At the break's end, a 20-second return ramp shows the re-entry note (idea 2) before the timer restarts. As an option, users can tag a break as off-phone (walk, snack, stretch) and see over time, privately, how their next block went after each kind of break: personal evidence rather than rules.
   - Rests on: phone breaks did not restore performance (Kang & Kurtzberg 2019, single study); restorative activities (Smits 2025 discussion); opportunity-cost model of effort (Kurzban 2013).
   - Strength: **thin to moderate**.

10. **Repair, not reset, after a missed deal.** When a planned session is skipped, the next open shows a neutral repair screen: "Yesterday's deal didn't happen. Want a smaller first move today?" The screen shrinks the next segment and offers a one-line self-compassion reframe. No backlog shame, and outstanding borrowed time can be written off on the user's request. The app offers a fresh-start landmark ("new week starts Monday: re-plan then?") only when the user is already off-track, never as a way to defer an on-track task.
    - Rests on: self-forgiveness (Wohl 2010, thin), self-compassion increases study effort after failure (Breines & Chen 2012), procrastination as mood repair (Sirois & Pychyl 2013), fresh-start effect and its backfire mode (Dai et al. 2014/2015).
    - Strength: **moderate** for self-compassion; **thin** for self-forgiveness specifically.

11. **Study rooms as "starting company".** In rooms, a lightweight "starting together at :00" moment: everyone's first move is visible as a single line, then silence. Rooms are suggested for routine segments (practice problems, flashcards) rather than first drafts.
    - Rests on: body doubling used mainly for initiation and isolation (survey evidence), small positive VR study vs a null controlled study, social facilitation helps simple and well-learned tasks (Bond & Titus 1983).
    - Strength: **thin**. Instrument it and treat it as an experiment.

12. **Always-visible external time.** On the lock screen and in a widget: elapsed and remaining time as a ring fill, plus optional gentle haptic checkpoints at 50% and 90% of a block and of a break. This externalizes time at the point of performance.
    - Rests on: timing deficits in ADHD (Marx et al. 2022 meta-analysis; adult evidence mixed), Barkley's externalization model.
    - Strength: **moderate** that the deficit exists; **thin** that visual timers improve outcomes. A cheap, low-risk default.
